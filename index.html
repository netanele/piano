    Drills

    Fast fingers, steady rhythm

    Letters tell you the note, so two skills are left: finding the key without hunting, and keeping time. One short round of each a day; accuracy first, then speed.

    Warm-ups and reading

    A five-finger warm-up for each nigun's key, and a new short tune every day to read once, slowly, without stopping. It trains reading, because the nigunim soon live in your memory.

    1. 1Set up your piano and yourself

      • Pick the main grand piano sound. Switch off rhythm, accompaniment, split and layer. Touch sensitivity: normal.
      • Bench height: forearms level with the keys. Sit on the front half of the bench, centred on Middle C.
      • Hand shape: curved fingers, as if holding a ball. Play on the fingertips; the thumb on its side corner. Wrists level and loose.
      • Fingers are numbered the same on both hands: thumb 1, index 2, middle 3, ring 4, pinky 5.
      • Put this page on the music stand. Turn your phone sideways for bigger notes. The screen stays awake while you practise.

    2. 2The keys

      Black keys come in groups of two and three. C is the white key just left of each pair. From C the white keys go C D E F G A B, then C again. Middle C is the 4th C from the left on an 88-key piano.

      A ♯ (sharp) is the black key just to the right of the letter; a ♭ (flat) is the black key just to the left. In the nigunim you meet G♯, C♯, F♯, D♯ and B♭ often.

    3. 3Reading a letter-note sheet: five things

      • The letter inside the note is the key.
      • Height and colour say which one of that letter: higher on the page = further right. Each octave has a colour, from one C up to the next B.
      • The shape says how long: filled notes are short, open notes are long (see Rhythm).
      • Small numbers above the notes are fingers. They appear where your hand moves to a new position.
      • Letters above the staff (Am, Dm, E) are chords for your left hand.
      C2 octaveC3C4 (Middle C)C5C6

      Read the shape of the melody, not only the letters: a step is the next key, a skip jumps one key. While you play a note, your eyes are already on the next one.

    4. 4Rhythm: the part letters don't do for you

      The top number of the time signature is the number of beats in a bar: 4 (most nigunim here), 2 (Daled Bavos) or 3. Count out loud. A dot adds half again. A rest is a silence of the same length.

      Sixteenths (two beams) are four to a beat: count “1 e & a”. A dotted eighth + sixteenth is a long-short “daa-di”, very common in nigunim. A tie (curved line between two same notes) means hold, don't play again. A pickup is a short first bar: the count-in counts the missing beats for you. A 3 over a group of notes is a triplet: three notes in the time of two. When a new time signature such as 3/4 appears in the middle of the music, the bars from there on have that many beats until it changes again (Pitchu Li and Chamol have a few).

    5. 5Signs: sharps for the whole bar, repeats and endings

      • A ♯ or ♭ lasts until the end of its bar. A ♮ (natural) cancels it. The letter inside the note always shows the real key anyway.
      • Section letters in a box (A, B, C) mark the parts of a nigun. Nigunim are built from parts that repeat.
      • Repeat signs (two dots by a thick line) mean: go back and play it again. 1. and 2. endings: the first time play ending 1, the second time skip it and play ending 2.

    6. 6Nigun scales under your five fingers

      Most nigunim are in minor or in freygish (Ahava Rabbah), the scale with the big step that gives them their longing sound. Each sits under one hand position, so learn the shape before the song.

    7. 7Chords: how they are built, and how the left hand plays them

      A chord is three notes played together. Start on the chord's letter (the root), then play one, skip one, play one, skip one, play one: A–C–E is A minor. The letter above the staff tells your left hand which chord to play, from that beat until the next letter.

      Major or minor? Count every key, white and black, from the root. Major: up 4, then up 3 (A–C♯–E). Minor, written with an m: up 3, then up 4 (A–C–E). Only the middle note changes, and you can hear it: major sounds bright, minor sounds dark.

      Seventh chords add one more note, two keys below the next root: A7 = A C♯ E G. They lead strongly back home (A7 → Dm, E7 → Am). The left hand plays root, third and seventh and leaves out the fifth.

      Same notes, different order. D–F–A can also be played F–A–D or A–D–F (inversions). In the Chord library you tick the shape you want for each chord; the player, the drills and the examples on this page then always play that shape.

      The chords of your nigunim. Most of them go home and away and home again. Play each one slowly, saying the names out loud:

      Grow the left hand in steps (Helpers → Left hand in the player):

      1. App plays: you play the melody and hear the chords.
      2. Root: one low note, the chord's letter. Say the chord names aloud.
      3. Root + 5th: add the note five white keys up (pinky and thumb).
      4. Chord: three notes, in the shape you ticked in the Chord library.
      5. Oom-pah: a low root on the strong beat, the chord on the others. Lively nigunim love it.
      6. Broken: the chord notes one at a time (low, middle, high, middle). Good for slow, singing nigunim.

    8. 8The map: every note above its key

      Same letter, different height and colour: follow the line down to the key. Tap a note or a key. On a phone, scroll sideways.

    9. 9How to practise (what the research says)

      • Hear it, sing it, then play it.Listening first and singing along speeds up learning, and nigunim are sung music.
      • Clean repetitions, not minutes.In a piano study, the share of correct run-throughs predicted the next day's playing; total time did not. Slow down until it is clean, then count 3 clean in a row.
      • Small chunks.One phrase (two bars) at a time, then join it to the next.
      • Speed up only when it's clean.+5% after 3 clean runs, −10% after two messy ones. Auto tempo does this for you.
      • Short and daily.20 minutes a day beats two hours once a week; sleep finishes the learning. Expect it to feel easier tomorrow.
      • Mix once you can play it.Switching between parts feels harder and sticks better.
      • Take helpers away.Key glow and finger numbers help you start but make you lean on them. Fade them as each part gets clean. The letters stay.
      • End by replaying what you started with.It protects the first thing you learned from the next one.
      • A missed day is fine.Aim for 5 days a week. Habits take about two months to settle.
